# Earth Sensors Lab — Executive Summary

Earth Sensors Lab is an accessible STEAM research and learning laboratory where students design, build, test, operate, and explain instruments for observing the Earth, atmosphere, living environment, and sky.

## Who

Earth Sensors Lab is designed for mixed-ability student cohorts. Disabled students are specifically encouraged to participate as designers, builders, researchers, and decision-makers, not as recipients of accommodations after a system has been built.

Students work individually and in teams according to interests and abilities. Teachers, engineers, artists, scientists, accessibility researchers, university researchers, community members, and institutional partners may support individual projects.

## What

Students build scientific instruments and the shared infrastructure required to operate them. The initial program includes:

1. Renewable Energy & Storage System
2. Soil Observatory
3. Microclimate Station
4. Seismic & Vibration Station
5. Air & Atmosphere Station
6. Light & Spectrum Instrument
7. Magnetic Field Observatory
8. Radio Sky Telescope
9. Cosmic Particle Detector

Each project includes a curriculum, research questions, scientific principles, parts list, schematic, technical drawing, accessible text/ASCII representation, build instructions, calibration procedure, data protocol, accessibility requirements, installation instructions, workshop activity, and Schema Card.

Students do not simply assemble kits. They learn why an instrument works, how its components work, how to construct it, how to determine whether it is working correctly, what its measurements mean, and what cannot responsibly be concluded from those measurements.

## Where

The instruments operate together within an accessible garden, greenhouse, school, university, museum, library, or community research site.

The garden or greenhouse functions as an **Earth Sensors Observatory**: a shared environment where different student teams investigate different phenomena within the same place.

Some instruments observe downward into soil and ground systems. Others observe plants and the immediate environment. Others examine air, weather, light, electromagnetic phenomena, the radio sky, and particles reaching Earth from space.

## How

Students follow a common research and engineering cycle:

**QUESTION → DESIGN → BUILD → CALIBRATE → DEPLOY → OBSERVE → RECORD → INTERROGATE → REPRESENT → SHARE → REDESIGN**

Every instrument must provide more than one way of accessing its observations. Depending on the phenomenon, data may be represented through numbers, text, image, sound, touch/haptics, tactile graphics, and speech. These are parallel representations of the same observation. A visual graph is not considered the primary scientific record with accessibility added afterward.

## Power

The observatory produces and manages part or all of its operating energy through student-built renewable-energy projects.

Students investigate solar generation, appropriate small-scale wind generation, batteries, capacitors and supercapacitors, charge control, power conversion, consumption, efficiency, storage, and energy budgeting. Energy production and consumption are themselves measured by the observatory.

Students therefore learn not only how to build an instrument, but what it takes to keep an instrument operating.

## Omoluabi

Every instrument, curriculum, observation, experiment, representation, rule, and finding uses structured identifiers and Schema Cards compatible with the Small Systems Lab rules-based system.

This allows Omoluabi to operate as an interrogation and learning interface across Earth Sensors Lab. A student can ask about an observation, and the system can trace relevant instruments, calibration records, rules, related observations, evidence, uncertainty, curriculum material, and alternative accessible representations.

Omoluabi does not replace observation or student reasoning. It helps students interrogate the evidence produced by the system.

## What Students Produce

Each cohort contributes to a growing Earth Sensors Observatory. The final outcome is larger than a collection of individual classroom projects.

Students produce working instruments, documented experiments, environmental observations, accessible data representations, renewable infrastructure, an evolving garden/observatory, and a public installation.

At designated points, the observatory opens to families, schools, researchers, and community members. Students operate the installation, explain their instruments, demonstrate their observations, answer questions, and lead hands-on workshops.

Public interaction becomes part of the next design cycle: students document what worked, what failed, what people could or could not understand or access, and what should be redesigned.
