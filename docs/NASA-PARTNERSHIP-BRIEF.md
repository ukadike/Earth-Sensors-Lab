# Earth Sensors Lab
## NASA-Facing Partnership Brief

**Earth Sensors Lab (ESL)** is an accessibility-first STEAM research and learning laboratory in which mixed-ability student cohorts design, build, calibrate, operate, and publicly explain instruments for observing Earth systems, the atmosphere, and the space environment.

Students begin with a shared garden or greenhouse observatory and learn science through the construction of real instruments: soil and water sensors, microclimate and air-quality stations, vibration/seismic instruments, light and spectral tools, magnetometers, radio-sky receivers, and eventually particle detectors. Renewable-energy generation, storage, and power telemetry are part of the observatory itself.

The program follows one common scientific and engineering cycle:

**QUESTION → DESIGN → BUILD → CALIBRATE → DEPLOY → OBSERVE → RECORD → INTERROGATE → REPRESENT → SHARE → REDESIGN**

## Why NASA

Earth Sensors Lab is designed around a continuous Earth-to-space learning pathway. NASA science, mission context, datasets, citizen-science precedents, and technical expertise can give students authentic connections between local observations and larger Earth and space systems.

Particularly strong points of connection include:

- **Earth Science:** soil, water, atmosphere, weather, living systems, environmental change;
- **Heliophysics:** magnetometry, space weather, solar radio observation, Earth's electromagnetic environment;
- **Astronomy and space science:** radio astronomy, spectroscopy, cosmic-particle observation;
- **Engineering:** instrumentation, calibration, power, telemetry, data systems, iteration and failure analysis;
- **STEM workforce pathways:** students learn how scientists and engineers create evidence, not simply how to consume scientific information.

NASA Radio JOVE provides an important precedent for ESL's approach: students and amateur scientists can construct and operate radio telescopes/radio spectrographs, observe natural radio emissions from the Sun, Jupiter and the galactic background, and contribute observations to real science activities.

## Accessibility as Research Infrastructure

ESL is built for mixed-ability cohorts, with disabled students participating as designers and researchers from the beginning.

Scientific observations can be represented through visual graphs, accessible tables, sound, speech, tactile graphics, Braille/tactile labeling, and haptic mappings where appropriate. These are treated as sibling representations of one canonical observation rather than accommodations derived from a privileged visual version.

This creates a research opportunity as well as an access strategy: ESL can study how different sensory representations affect scientific pattern recognition, anomaly detection, explanation, collaboration, and learning.

## Instrument Literacy

Students do not simply assemble kits. Every instrument curriculum asks:

- What phenomenon are we observing?
- What detects it?
- How does the physical phenomenon become a signal?
- How does the signal become data?
- How is the instrument calibrated?
- What creates noise or error?
- What uncertainty remains?
- What evidence would be required to support a conclusion?
- How can another person independently understand or reproduce the observation?

The goal is to develop scientific and engineering judgment alongside technical skill.

## Community Observatory

Individual student projects become one shared Earth Sensors Observatory. Different teams observe different parts of the same environment and compare findings across time.

At designated points, the observatory opens as an accessible public installation. Students demonstrate instruments, explain measurements and uncertainty, lead workshops, and invite families and community members to experience environmental and space data through multiple sensory modes.

Public feedback enters the next engineering cycle: students document failures, access barriers, misunderstandings, and opportunities for redesign.

## Research Directions

ESL is developing research questions in four areas:

1. **Low-cost observatory validity:** when can student-built instruments produce scientifically useful observations?
2. **Instrument literacy:** how does building and calibrating an instrument change understanding of evidence and uncertainty?
3. **Accessible scientific reasoning:** how do sonic, tactile, haptic, spoken, visual and tabular representations affect scientific interpretation?
4. **Networked observatories:** what becomes possible when multiple schools or communities operate interoperable observation sites using shared protocols and schemas?

## Small Systems Lab Infrastructure

Every instrument, curriculum, calibration, observation, experiment, representation, and finding carries structured metadata and provenance through the Small Systems Lab Schema Card system.

Omoluabi, a rules-based interrogation layer, can use those records to help students trace an observation back through its instrument, calibration, protocol, uncertainty, related evidence, and accessible representations. It is not positioned as an autonomous scientific authority and is prohibited from inventing missing measurements or converting uncertainty into fact.

## What a NASA Relationship Could Enable

Depending on the appropriate NASA program or partnership mechanism, ESL would value collaboration around:

- NASA mission and dataset integration;
- scientist and engineer consultation;
- technical review of Earth-to-space instrument curricula;
- Radio JOVE and heliophysics pathways;
- student magnetometry and space-weather observation;
- accessible scientific-data representation;
- career exposure and STEM workforce pathways;
- evaluation of authentic science experiences;
- connections to relevant NASA citizen-science and Science Activation communities;
- development of openly documented resources that other schools and community sites can reuse.

Earth Sensors Lab does **not** claim NASA endorsement or partnership. The purpose of this brief is to identify substantive areas in which ESL's accessibility-first instrument-building model may align with NASA science, engineering, STEM engagement, and public participation goals.
