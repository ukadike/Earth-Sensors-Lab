# Earth Sensors Lab — NASA Readiness Framework

**Status:** program alignment document; not a claim of NASA endorsement, certification, funding, or partnership.

Earth Sensors Lab (ESL) is being developed so that its research, learning, accessibility, and public-engagement architecture can be legible to NASA scientists, engineers, STEM-engagement teams, university collaborators, and prospective funders.

NASA does not provide a single universal "NASA compliant" designation for an independent program of this kind. Readiness therefore means that ESL can demonstrate clear mission relevance, authentic science and engineering practice, transparent evidence, accessible participation, public value, and a defined pathway for using NASA science, data, expertise, and established citizen-science precedents where appropriate.

## 1. NASA-Relevant Program Proposition

Earth Sensors Lab is an accessibility-first student observatory and instrument-building research program. Mixed-ability student cohorts design, build, calibrate, deploy, operate, interrogate, and publicly explain instruments that observe coupled systems from soil and living environments through atmosphere, electromagnetism, the radio sky, and particle phenomena.

The program connects four activities that are often separated:

1. **STEM learning and workforce development** — students learn science and engineering by constructing and maintaining instruments.
2. **Authentic observation and research practice** — calibration, provenance, uncertainty, quality assurance, longitudinal data, and reproducibility are part of the curriculum.
3. **Accessibility research and inclusive instrument design** — scientific observations can be represented visually, numerically, sonically, through speech, tactile graphics, and haptics as sibling representations of the same canonical observation.
4. **Public science and creative technology** — student research becomes an accessible observatory installation, demonstration environment, and community workshop platform.

## 2. Earth-to-Space Science Architecture

ESL uses a coherent observation progression rather than attaching "space" to an unrelated garden program.

```text
SOIL / WATER / LIVING SYSTEMS
            |
            v
      LOCAL ATMOSPHERE
            |
            v
      LIGHT / SPECTRA
            |
            v
 EARTH'S MAGNETIC ENVIRONMENT
            |
            v
       RADIO SKY
            |
            v
     COSMIC PARTICLES
```

The garden or greenhouse is the first bounded observatory: a place where students can repeatedly measure a shared environment, compare instruments, observe change, and learn how Earth systems interact. The program then extends the same instrument-literacy method outward toward heliophysics, astronomy, and the space environment.

## 4. NASA Engagement Track

This track is evaluated primarily through learning, participation, skill development, accessibility, public engagement, and pathways into STEM.

Possible outputs include:

- student-built instruments;
- documented engineering-design cycles;
- NASA-data and mission-linked curriculum;
- student research notebooks;
- accessible public installations;
- family/community observatory days;
- student-led build workshops;
- career exposure and scientist/engineer engagement;
- reusable openly documented learning resources;
- teacher/facilitator professional development;
- evaluation of learning, access, participation, and persistence.

ESL should prioritize authentic NASA content over NASA-themed decoration. Where a NASA relationship is proposed, the relevant instrument or curriculum should identify the NASA mission, dataset, scientist/engineer expertise, citizen-science project, or technical concept that makes the relationship substantive.

## 5. NASA Research Track

A research proposal must not rely on educational value alone. A research track must state a research question or hypothesis, define the observation and instrument, document calibration and quality controls, preserve provenance, report uncertainty, define analysis methods, and identify what scientific conclusion the evidence could support.

Potential ESL research families include:

### Low-Cost Observatory Validity
Under what calibration, collocation, environmental, and QA conditions can student-built or low-cost instruments yield scientifically useful observations?

### Accessible Scientific Reasoning
How do visual, sonic, tactile, haptic, spoken, and tabular representations affect anomaly detection, pattern recognition, scientific explanation, and collaborative reasoning among disabled and nondisabled learners?

### Instrument Literacy
How does designing and constructing an instrument affect a learner's understanding of measurement, error, uncertainty, evidence, and scientific inference compared with using a sealed commercial instrument?

### Networked Student Observatories
What scientific and learning value emerges when multiple sites use interoperable instruments, shared schemas, and comparable protocols to observe environmental or space-weather phenomena?

### Energy Cost of Observation
How much energy is required to sense, compute, store, represent, and communicate an observation, and how can a student observatory manage that energy as a measurable system constraint?

## 6. NASA Partnership Readiness

NASA Office of STEM Engagement currently identifies nonprofits, educational institutions, industry, and other organizations as potential collaborators for high-impact STEM activities. NASA prioritizes real-world problem solving, career pathways, NASA datasets, current mission information, publicly accessible materials, and broad student reach. Formal collaboration may use mechanisms including Space Act Agreements depending on the relationship; a partnership mechanism is distinct from a grant or procurement contract.

Before approaching NASA for a formal relationship, ESL should be able to present:

- a concise executive summary;
- defined target learners and age ranges;
- an accessibility and inclusion model;
- named instrument/curriculum modules;
- a pilot or research-site model;
- a NASA alignment matrix;
- explicit NASA assets requested (data, expertise, mission context, review, speakers, facilities where relevant);
- explicit value ESL contributes to NASA's mission and audiences;
- safeguarding, consent, privacy, and data policies;
- evaluation measures;
- open/public dissemination plan where appropriate;
- realistic staffing, schedule, budget, and partner roles;
- status labels that distinguish proposed work from built/tested/validated work.

## 7. Technical and Scientific Readiness Rules

Every NASA-facing ESL instrument should answer:

1. What physical phenomenon is being observed?
2. What sensor or transducer detects it?
3. What is the raw signal?
4. How is the signal converted into data?
5. What units are reported?
6. How is the instrument calibrated?
7. What reference or comparison method is used?
8. What environmental or technical factors can confound the measurement?
9. How is uncertainty represented?
10. What provenance is preserved?
11. What claims can the data support?
12. What claims can the data not support?
13. How can the same observation be accessed without vision, hearing, or fine-motor assumptions where feasible?
14. What NASA science, dataset, mission, precedent, or technical domain is genuinely relevant?

## 8. Radio Astronomy: Model NASA Module

NASA Radio JOVE is a particularly strong precedent for ESL because participants can build and operate radio telescopes/radio spectrographs and collect observations of natural radio emissions associated with the Sun, Jupiter, Earth's ionosphere, and the galactic background.

An ESL radio module should therefore teach:

```text
ELECTROMAGNETIC PHENOMENON
          |
          v
       ANTENNA
          |
          v
       RECEIVER
          |
          v
 DIGITAL / SPECTRAL DATA
          |
    +-----+------+-------+
    |            |       |
    v            v       v
 VISUAL        SONIC   TABULAR/TEXT
 SPECTRUM      MAPPING   DATA
    \            |       /
     +-----------+------+
                 |
                 v
        SAME OBSERVATION RECORD
```

Students must also learn radio-frequency interference, observation windows, antenna placement, failed observations, and the difference between detecting a signal and responsibly identifying its source.

## 9. Accessibility as Scientific Infrastructure

Accessibility is not a separate outreach deliverable. It is an instrument-design and research requirement.

Every canonical observation may have multiple rule-based representations. For example:

```text
ESL-OBS-00438
      |
      +--> numerical values + units
      +--> accessible table
      +--> visual graph
      +--> sonification
      +--> spoken description
      +--> tactile graphic
      +--> haptic mapping (when scientifically and ergonomically useful)
```

Each representation must point back to the same underlying observation and document the transformation rule used. A sonification or tactile representation must not silently alter scientific meaning for aesthetic effect.

Disabled students should participate in the design, testing, critique, and redesign of these interfaces. Accessibility validation must not be claimed until the relevant participants and protocol support that claim.

## 10. Omoluabi and NASA-Facing Evidence

Omoluabi is not positioned as an autonomous scientific authority. It operates over the Small Systems Lab schema and rules layer so that students and researchers can interrogate provenance and relationships.

A valid trace may be:

```text
QUESTION
   |
   v
OBSERVATION
   |
   +--> INSTRUMENT
   |       |
   |       +--> SENSOR / TRANSDUCER
   |       +--> CALIBRATION RECORD
   |       +--> STATUS
   |
   +--> PROTOCOL
   +--> LOCATION / TIME
   +--> UNCERTAINTY
   +--> RELATED OBSERVATIONS
   +--> REPRESENTATION RULES
   +--> EVIDENCE / LIMITATIONS
```

Rules prohibit Omoluabi from inventing missing measurements, converting uncertainty into fact, or replacing scientific judgment.

## 11. Creative Technology and Public Science

ESL treats exhibition and creative technology as methods for making scientific processes publicly inspectable, not as decoration added after research.

The public observatory can allow visitors to:

- encounter live and historical measurements;
- compare Earth, atmosphere, and sky instruments;
- hear a scientifically defined sonification;
- examine tactile data;
- feel an appropriate haptic mapping;
- inspect how an instrument was built and calibrated;
- ask students to explain uncertainty and competing interpretations;
- build a simple instrument in a workshop;
- understand how the observatory powers itself.

This creates a credible bridge among science, engineering, accessibility, art, creative technology, and public engagement.

## 12. Funding Readiness

ESL should maintain modular proposal language rather than one generic funding pitch.

A NASA STEM-engagement proposal may foreground authentic NASA science, student engineering, workforce pathways, public resources, accessibility, and community reach.

A NASA science/research proposal must foreground the research question, scientific contribution, methodology, instrument validity, data management, analysis, uncertainty, and publication/dissemination plan.

An accessibility/HCI proposal may foreground multimodal scientific reasoning, disabled co-design, equivalent data representations, usability, and accessible instrumentation.

An arts/creative-technology proposal may foreground the observatory as a public research installation while preserving the scientific provenance of every represented observation.

An environmental or climate funder may foreground community observation, environmental literacy, local data, energy, living systems, and stewardship.

The core program does not change. The proposal identifies which validated part of the same system addresses the funder's mission.

## 13. Immediate Readiness Priorities

Before representing ESL as pilot-ready, complete the following:

1. Build and document the first instrument cohort: soil moisture, microclimate, air particles, energy telemetry, mechanical seismograph, and multimodal data translator.
2. Produce complete Schema Cards and calibration records for those instruments.
3. Establish a NASA-linked radio-sky curriculum using Radio JOVE as a technical/scientific precedent rather than claiming NASA affiliation.
4. Develop the student magnetometer module and space-weather curriculum.
5. Establish the accessible-observation research protocol with disabled participants/advisors.
6. Define the observatory data model and Omoluabi traceability rules.
7. Establish evaluation metrics for learning, engineering skill, scientific reasoning, accessibility, and public engagement.
8. Document which NASA datasets, missions, resources, citizen-science programs, or experts would add substantive value to each module.
9. Prepare a one-page NASA-facing partnership brief.
10. Never use NASA branding or imply NASA endorsement without authorization.
