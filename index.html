Overview

A community observatory built around access

Earth Sensors Lab is a public-interest research initiative under Small Systems Lab. It studies how learners observe environmental and planetary systems when scientific data can be seen, heard, touched, spoken, questioned, and connected to place.

The lab begins with the immediate environment — soil, water, heat, air, sound, plants, weather, and energy — and extends that same observational logic upward to the atmosphere and sky. The goal is not simply to teach STEM tools. It is to widen who gets to observe, measure, interpret, build instruments, and explain the world.

Earth → Atmosphere → Space

One learning system can connect garden sensors, environmental data, renewable energy, optical astronomy, radio observation, and multisensory interpretation. The instrument pathway is designed to grow with the learner and the site.

Playbook

How the lab works

The playbook translates the research vision into a repeatable lab model: observe first, introduce instruments second, make data available in more than one sensory form, document uncertainty, and return interpretation to the learner and community.

Observe before measuring

Learners begin with human observation and questions before introducing a sensor. Measurement should deepen attention, not replace it.

Multiple ways to know

Charts are only one representation. Data may also become sound, tactile form, spoken description, physical movement, or collaborative interpretation.

Build the observatory

Students learn the system by assembling it: sensors, power, housings, calibration, data pathways, accessibility layers, and field documentation.

Keep humans in judgment

Automation can help flag patterns or explain readings, but evidence, uncertainty, context, and scientific claims remain visible and reviewable.

Instruments

An instrument ladder from the garden to the sky

The lab treats instruments as a progression rather than a shopping list. Each step introduces a new scale of observation while preserving low-cost, repairable, accessible, and teachable design.

01

Garden sensing

Soil moisture, temperature, humidity, light, water, air, and plant observation.

02

Environmental station

Weather, sound, air quality, energy, field logging, and local microclimate patterns.

03

Optical sky observation

Human-visible sky observation, telescope practice, imaging, time, direction, and celestial movement.

04

Radio observation

Radio astronomy concepts, antennas, signal, interference, spectra, and forms of observation beyond sight.

Accessibility

Accessibility is part of the instrument

If a measurement can only be read visually, the scientific system is incomplete. Earth Sensors Lab treats accessibility as a method for creating richer forms of observation, not as a layer added after the science is finished.

See

High-contrast charts, legible interfaces, scalable text, visual patterning, and clear spatial relationships.

Hear

Sonification, spoken readings, audio description, captions, and transcripts.

Touch

Tactile diagrams, physical models, manipulable components, and nonvisual representations of change.

Navigate

Keyboard access, screen-reader-ready structure, predictable information architecture, and low-bandwidth/offline options.

NASA / Space

A credible pathway from community science to space science

The space-facing work does not begin by pretending Earth Sensors Lab is already a NASA program. It begins with a readiness framework: clear learning outcomes, documented instrument standards, accessible data practices, curriculum, research methods, partnership language, and an Earth-to-space progression that can support future institutional collaboration.

That creates a concrete basis for conversations with science educators, universities, observatories, museums, community organizations, and eventually NASA-facing education and citizen-science programs.

Partnerships

Schools and institutions participate as research partners

Earth Sensors Lab is designed for collaboration rather than product delivery. A pilot site can become a place where students, teachers, researchers, accessibility advisors, technologists, and community members jointly test how an observatory should work.

Schools

Curriculum pilots, school gardens, student-built observatories, teacher collaboration, and longitudinal observation.

Universities + labs

Research design, instrument mentorship, evaluation, accessibility research, and technical exchange.

Museums + observatories

Public science interpretation, telescope access, informal learning, exhibitions, and community observing events.

Community partners

Local environmental questions, place-based knowledge, stewardship, public data, and community ownership.
